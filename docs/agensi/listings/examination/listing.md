## Full Description

```markdown
## What it does

Examines a learner the way a good oral exam does: one question at a time,
waits for a committed answer, and mixes prediction, explanation, edge cases,
comparison, and novel application.

Recognition is never treated as understanding. A learner who says "it's a
for loop" has not demonstrated that they know when *not* to use one.

## What makes it different from a generic quiz skill

- **One question at a time, and it waits.** A learner who is handed four
  questions answers the easiest one and moves on.
- **It withholds the answer until commitment.** Guessing first and checking
  afterwards is not retrieval practice, and it feels like learning anyway.
- **It corrects the smallest misconception first.** Fixing a big one first
  usually means the learner rebuilds the whole model around a wrong
  foundation.
- **It separates terminology errors from conceptual errors.** They need
  different corrections, and confusing them teaches the wrong thing.
- **It probes vague language.** "It just works" gets asked to define what
  "it" is, with a concrete example.

## The final assessment

When the learner says they are done, the skill delivers a concise assessment:
confidence rating, strongest part, weakest part, the missing concept, one
follow-up question, and one recommended exercise. It corrects only what
needs correcting and asks the learner to restate the repaired idea — which
is the only test that the repair stuck.

## Who it is for

- Teachers and tutors checking real understanding rather than recall
- Learners who want to be examined properly
- Coding assistants acting as an examiner rather than a pair-programmer

## What is in the package

- `SKILL.md` — the examination behaviour
- `README.md` — entry points and examples
```

Test whether I actually understand how recursion works. Don't just quiz me on

### The result (what their agent delivers)

```markdown
Let's do this properly. One question at a time, and I'll wait for your answer
before the next one. I'll also tell you when I'm asking for a prediction
rather than a definition, because prediction is the part that actually
reveals gaps.

## Question 1 — prediction

Here's a function:

```python
def count(n):
    if n == 0:
        return 0
    return 1 + count(n - 1)
```

**Before running it, predict the output of `count(-1)`.** Give me a number
or "infinite recursion" or "error" — commit to one.

*Why this one:* recursion bugs live in the base case, and a learner who
believes the base case is "always reached" will say `0` here without
thinking. The prediction separates that belief from the real one.

---

**How to answer this next one:** a prediction question means a single
committed answer, no hedging. A "why" question means a short explanation in
your own words. An application question means you write the thing, not
describe it. I'll tell you which kind each one is.

**What I am listening for across the session:** whether you reach for the
definition of recursion or for the control flow, and whether you can tell me
what a program does at the moment it is *not* executing the function you
are thinking of.

---

## What you get at the end

When you tell me you're done, here is the shape of the assessment — so you
know what we are aiming at:

> **Confidence:** 3/5
> **Strongest:** explaining the base case in your own words
> **Weakest:** predicting behaviour on inputs that break the assumption
> **Missing concept:** that a base case is a *guard*, and it has to be
> reachable from every path
> **Follow-up question:** in `count(-1)`, which call is the one that never
> returns?
> **Next exercise:** write a recursive sum that returns `0` immediately for
> negative `n` — then predict its output before you run it

And then one more thing: I ask you to restate the repaired idea in your own
words, because being told you were right is not the same as knowing why.
```

## Extra Details

**FAQ**

**Q: What does the final assessment contain?**
A: Confidence rating, strongest part, weakest part, the missing concept, one
follow-up question, and one recommended exercise.

**Q: Will it give me the answer?**
A: Not before you commit to one. The point of the format is that a guess
followed by an explanation is not retrieval practice.

**Q: How is this different from a quiz generator?**
A: A quiz generator produces questions and marks answers. This one adapts:
it asks for a prediction before a definition, probes vague terms with a
concrete example, distinguishes terminology mistakes from conceptual ones,
and corrects the smallest misconception first.

**Q: Can it assess code?**
A: Yes — it will ask you to predict what a piece of code does, explain it,
and apply the idea to a case you have not seen.
